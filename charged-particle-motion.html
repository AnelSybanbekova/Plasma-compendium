Charged Particle Motion in Magnetic Fields

Introduction

The motion of charged particles in electromagnetic fields is one of the fundamental topics in plasma physics. Since a plasma consists of charged particles, its behaviour is strongly influenced by electric and magnetic fields.

Understanding the motion of a single charged particle provides an important foundation for understanding collective plasma behaviour and magnetic confinement. In devices such as tokamaks and stellarators, magnetic fields are used to control the motion of charged particles and reduce their losses from the confined plasma.

The simplest important case is the motion of a charged particle in a uniform magnetic field. This problem leads to several fundamental concepts, including cyclotron motion, the Larmor radius, helical trajectories, and the guiding center approximation.

The Lorentz Force

The motion of a charged particle in an electromagnetic field is governed by the Lorentz force:

F⃗ = q(E⃗ + v⃗ × B⃗)

where q is the particle charge, E⃗ is the electric field, v⃗ is the particle velocity, and B⃗ is the magnetic field.

The Lorentz force consists of two contributions. The electric force is

F⃗E = qE⃗

while the magnetic force is

F⃗B = q(v⃗ × B⃗)

The electric force acts along the electric field and can change the kinetic energy of the particle. The magnetic force, however, is perpendicular to both the particle velocity and the magnetic field.

Therefore,

F⃗B · v⃗ = 0

and the magnetic field does no work on the particle. A static magnetic field can therefore change the direction of a particle's velocity without changing its kinetic energy.

Motion in a Uniform Magnetic Field

Consider a charged particle moving in a uniform and time-independent magnetic field, with no electric field:

E⃗ = 0,    B⃗ = constant

The equation of motion becomes

m dv⃗/dt = q(v⃗ × B⃗)

It is useful to decompose the particle velocity into two components: one parallel to the magnetic field and one perpendicular to it.

v⃗ = v⃗∥ + v⃗⊥

The parallel component is unaffected by the magnetic force because it is parallel to B⃗:

v⃗∥ × B⃗ = 0

The perpendicular component, on the other hand, experiences a magnetic force. This force is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity and changes its direction continuously.

As a result, the particle moves freely along the magnetic field while simultaneously undergoing circular motion in the plane perpendicular to the field.

Cyclotron Motion

Consider the special case in which the particle velocity is entirely perpendicular to the magnetic field:

v⃗ = v⃗⊥

The magnetic force then acts as the centripetal force required for circular motion:

|q|v⊥B = mv⊥2/rL

Solving for the orbit radius gives

rL = mv⊥ / |q|B

This radius is called the Larmor radius or gyroradius. It characterizes the size of the particle's circular orbit around the magnetic field line.

The angular frequency of the circular motion is

ωc = v⊥ / rL

Substituting the expression for the Larmor radius gives the cyclotron frequency:

ωc = |q|B / m

The corresponding cyclotron frequency in hertz is

fc = |q|B / 2πm

The cyclotron frequency depends on the particle charge, mass, and magnetic field strength. For a given magnetic field, electrons gyrate much faster than ions because their mass is much smaller.

Larmor Radius

The Larmor radius is one of the most important characteristic scales in magnetized plasma physics:

rL = mv⊥ / |q|B

The equation shows that the orbit becomes smaller when the magnetic field strength increases. It also becomes smaller for particles with a larger charge-to-mass ratio.

For a strongly magnetized plasma, the Larmor radius is much smaller than the characteristic spatial scale of the system:

rL ≪ L

where L is a characteristic length scale of the magnetic field or plasma.

This condition is particularly important for magnetic confinement. When the Larmor radius is small compared with the size of the confinement device, particles undergo many gyromotions before experiencing significant changes in the macroscopic magnetic field.

Helical Motion

In the general case, a charged particle has both parallel and perpendicular velocity components:

v⃗ = v⃗∥ + v⃗⊥

The perpendicular component produces circular motion around the magnetic field line, while the parallel component produces uniform motion along the field line.

The resulting trajectory is therefore a helix.

Helical motion = gyromotion + parallel motion

The pitch of the helical trajectory is the distance travelled along the magnetic field during one complete gyroperiod:

Tc = 2π / ωc

p = v∥Tc

or, equivalently,

p = 2πv∥ / ωc

If v∥ = 0, the trajectory becomes a circle. If v⊥ = 0, the particle moves along the magnetic field line without gyration.

Direction of Gyromotion

The direction of gyromotion depends on the sign of the particle charge. Positive and negative particles rotate in opposite directions around the same magnetic field.

This difference is particularly important in a plasma containing both electrons and ions. Although both species are affected by the same magnetic field, their opposite charges cause them to gyrate in opposite directions.

Their masses also differ by several orders of magnitude. Consequently, electrons have a much higher cyclotron frequency and, for comparable perpendicular velocities, a much smaller Larmor radius than ions.

The Guiding Center

The cyclotron motion of a charged particle is usually much faster than the slower motion of the center of its orbit. Instead of following the complete circular trajectory, it is therefore useful to introduce the concept of the guiding center.

The guiding center represents the instantaneous center of the particle's gyromotion. In a perfectly uniform magnetic field, the guiding center follows the magnetic field line while the particle gyrates around it.

This provides a useful separation between two types of motion:

  • Fast gyromotion around the magnetic field line.
  • Slower motion of the guiding center.

In a real plasma, magnetic fields are rarely perfectly uniform. They can have spatial gradients, curvature, and electric fields. Under these conditions, the guiding center itself can move across magnetic field lines.

This slower motion is known as particle drift and becomes particularly important in magnetic confinement devices.

Magnetic Moment

For a charged particle undergoing gyromotion in a slowly varying magnetic field, another useful quantity is the magnetic moment:

μ = mv⊥2 / 2B

Under appropriate adiabatic conditions, this quantity is approximately conserved. This means that changes in the magnetic field can influence the particle's perpendicular and parallel motion.

The magnetic moment provides a connection between the microscopic motion of individual particles and macroscopic effects such as magnetic mirroring and particle trapping.

Magnetic Mirror Effect

Consider a particle moving through a magnetic field whose strength gradually increases. If the magnetic moment remains approximately constant, an increase in B must be accompanied by a corresponding change in the perpendicular kinetic energy.

Since the total kinetic energy remains constant in a static magnetic field, the parallel velocity can decrease as the perpendicular velocity increases.

Eventually, the parallel velocity can become zero. The particle then reverses its direction and moves back toward the region of weaker magnetic field.

This phenomenon is known as the magnetic mirror effect.

μ = mv⊥2 / 2B ≈ constant

The magnetic mirror effect is one of the basic principles behind magnetic mirror confinement and also plays an important role in understanding particle trapping in non-uniform magnetic fields.

Charged Particle Motion and Magnetic Confinement

The motion described above provides the microscopic basis for magnetic confinement.

A strong magnetic field causes charged particles to gyrate around magnetic field lines with a characteristic Larmor radius. If this radius is much smaller than the dimensions of the plasma, the particle is strongly magnetized.

However, a magnetic field does not simply force particles to remain exactly on a single field line. In realistic magnetic confinement systems, the field has curvature and spatial gradients, and electric fields may also be present.

These effects cause the guiding centers of particles to move across magnetic field lines. Understanding these motions is therefore essential for understanding particle losses, plasma transport, and confinement quality.

This leads naturally to the next important topic in magnetized plasma physics: particle drifts.

Key Equations

The most important equations from this chapter are summarized below.

F⃗ = q(E⃗ + v⃗ × B⃗)

rL = mv⊥ / |q|B

ωc = |q|B / m

fc = |q|B / 2πm

Tc = 2π / ωc

p = 2πv∥ / ωc

μ = mv⊥2 / 2B

rL ≪ L

Summary

  • The motion of a charged particle in electromagnetic fields is governed by the Lorentz force.
  • A magnetic field changes the direction of particle motion but does not perform work on the particle.
  • The velocity can be decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field.
  • The perpendicular component produces cyclotron motion around the magnetic field line.
  • The characteristic radius of this motion is the Larmor radius.
  • The characteristic angular frequency is the cyclotron frequency.
  • The combination of gyromotion and parallel motion produces a helical trajectory.
  • The guiding center describes the slower motion of the center of the gyro-orbit.
  • In non-uniform electromagnetic fields, the guiding center can drift across magnetic field lines.
  • These effects form the microscopic foundation for understanding magnetic confinement and plasma transport.

Connection to the Next Topic

The idealized motion in a uniform magnetic field is only the beginning. Real magnetic confinement systems contain curved and non-uniform magnetic fields. These variations produce additional motions of the guiding center known as particle drifts.

The next step is therefore to study how electric fields, magnetic-field gradients, and magnetic-field curvature influence the motion of charged particles in magnetized plasmas.
