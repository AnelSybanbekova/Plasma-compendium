Plasma Equilibrium and Stability

Introduction

Magnetic confinement provides a way to keep a hot plasma away from material surfaces, but confinement alone is not sufficient. A plasma is a dynamic electromagnetic system containing charged particles, electric currents, pressure gradients, and magnetic fields.

For a confined plasma to remain in a controlled state, these forces must be balanced. This condition is known as plasma equilibrium.

However, an equilibrium does not necessarily mean that the plasma will remain stable. Small perturbations can grow and distort the plasma, potentially leading to increased particle and energy losses or even a complete loss of confinement.

This chapter introduces the basic physics of plasma equilibrium and the main types of instabilities encountered in magnetically confined plasmas.

1. What Is Plasma Equilibrium?

An equilibrium is a state in which the macroscopic forces acting on the plasma are balanced.

For a magnetized plasma, an important competition exists between the plasma pressure and the magnetic forces that confine it.

In a simplified magnetohydrodynamic description, the equilibrium condition can be written as

J × B = ∇p

where

  • J is the plasma current density,
  • B is the magnetic field,
  • p is the plasma pressure.

This equation expresses a fundamental idea: the magnetic force must balance the pressure gradient inside the plasma.

If the plasma pressure is higher in one region than another, the resulting pressure gradient tends to push the plasma outward. The magnetic field must provide a force capable of balancing this tendency.

2. Plasma Pressure

Plasma pressure arises from the thermal motion of charged particles. For a simple isotropic plasma, the total pressure can be approximated as

p = nekTe + nikTi

where

  • ne is the electron density,
  • ni is the ion density,
  • Te is the electron temperature,
  • Ti is the ion temperature,
  • k is the Boltzmann constant.

For a quasi-neutral plasma,

ne ≈ ni

and for comparable electron and ion temperatures, the pressure can become very large.

In fusion plasmas, temperatures can reach tens or hundreds of millions of kelvin. Consequently, maintaining equilibrium requires strong magnetic fields and carefully controlled plasma conditions.

3. Magnetic Pressure

A magnetic field itself carries energy and can exert an effective pressure on the plasma.

The magnetic pressure is

pB = B2 / 2μ0

where μ0 is the permeability of free space.

A stronger magnetic field therefore provides a larger magnetic pressure. This is one of the fundamental reasons why strong magnetic fields are required for fusion confinement.

The competition between plasma pressure and magnetic pressure leads to one of the most important dimensionless parameters in fusion plasma physics: plasma beta.

4. Plasma Beta

Plasma beta, β, is defined as the ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure:

β = p / pB

Using the expression for magnetic pressure gives

β = 2μ0p / B2

The value of β provides an indication of how effectively the magnetic field is being used to confine plasma pressure.

A low-beta plasma has

β ≪ 1

meaning that the magnetic pressure is much larger than the plasma pressure.

A higher-beta plasma has a larger plasma pressure relative to the magnetic field.

From the perspective of fusion energy, high beta is attractive because it means that a larger plasma pressure can be supported for a given magnetic field strength.

However, increasing beta can also make the plasma more susceptible to certain instabilities. Therefore, beta is closely connected to both fusion performance and plasma stability.

5. Force Balance in Magnetized Plasma

The simplified equilibrium equation

J × B = ∇p

can be understood physically as a balance between two effects.

The pressure gradient tends to push plasma from regions of high pressure toward regions of lower pressure.

The electromagnetic force associated with the plasma current and magnetic field acts to confine and shape the plasma.

A magnetically confined plasma therefore exists in a state of continuous force balance rather than being completely motionless.

This distinction is important: equilibrium does not mean that individual particles are stationary. Particles continue to undergo gyromotion, drifts, collisions, and thermal motion even when the plasma is in macroscopic equilibrium.

6. Magnetic Flux Surfaces

In toroidal confinement devices, equilibrium is often described using magnetic flux surfaces.

These are nested surfaces on which magnetic field lines remain approximately confined.

The plasma pressure is commonly organized approximately as a function of magnetic flux:

p = p(ψ)

where ψ represents a magnetic flux coordinate.

The central region of the plasma is associated with high pressure, while the pressure generally decreases toward the plasma edge.

The magnetic surfaces therefore provide a geometric framework for describing the equilibrium structure of the confined plasma.

7. What Is Plasma Stability?

An equilibrium may exist without being stable.

To understand stability, imagine slightly displacing a portion of the plasma from its equilibrium position.

Three general outcomes are possible:

  • Stable: the perturbation decreases or remains bounded.
  • Marginally stable: the perturbation remains approximately constant.
  • Unstable: the perturbation grows with time.

A useful conceptual expression for an unstable perturbation is

δ(t) ∝ eγt

where γ is the growth rate.

If

γ > 0

the perturbation grows and the equilibrium is unstable.

If

γ < 0

the perturbation decays and the system tends to return toward equilibrium.

8. Sources of Plasma Instability

Plasma instabilities can arise from several physical mechanisms.

Important sources include:

  • pressure gradients,
  • magnetic-field curvature,
  • plasma current,
  • velocity gradients,
  • magnetic reconnection,
  • interactions between plasma pressure and magnetic geometry.

Because plasma is an electromagnetic medium, perturbations in density, pressure, current, and magnetic field are coupled together.

This allows relatively small disturbances to develop into macroscopic instabilities.

9. Kink Instability

The kink instability is associated with distortions that bend or twist the plasma column.

Instead of remaining centered and symmetric, the plasma can develop a helical displacement.

In a current-carrying plasma, the plasma current itself contributes to the magnetic configuration. If the current and magnetic geometry are such that a perturbation can lower the system's magnetic energy, the perturbation may grow.

Kink modes are particularly important in tokamak physics because tokamaks rely on a substantial plasma current.

Controlling the current profile and magnetic-field geometry is therefore essential for maintaining stable operation.

10. Sausage Instability

The sausage instability changes the cross-sectional radius of a plasma column.

Some regions become wider while others become narrower, producing a shape resembling a string of sausages.

This is an example of an interchange-type deformation of the plasma configuration.

The instability illustrates how magnetic pressure and plasma pressure can interact with the geometry of the plasma.

11. Ballooning Instability

The ballooning instability is strongly related to plasma pressure gradients and unfavorable magnetic curvature.

A portion of the plasma can bulge outward, producing a deformation similar to an inflated balloon.

Ballooning modes are particularly relevant in high-beta plasmas because increasing plasma pressure strengthens the pressure-gradient drive for instability.

This creates an important connection between fusion performance and stability: increasing beta can improve the efficiency of magnetic confinement but may also make certain pressure-driven instabilities more likely.

12. Tearing Modes

A tearing mode is a type of instability associated with changes in magnetic topology and magnetic reconnection.

In ideal magnetohydrodynamics, magnetic field lines are effectively frozen into the plasma.

In regions where non-ideal effects become important, magnetic field lines can reconnect and change their topology.

This process can produce structures known as magnetic islands.

Magnetic reconnection → Magnetic islands → Modified transport

Large magnetic islands can degrade confinement by providing pathways through which particles and energy can move across magnetic surfaces.

13. Magnetic Islands

In an ideal equilibrium, magnetic surfaces are nested and smooth. Perturbations can distort these surfaces and produce island structures.

A magnetic island contains a set of magnetic field lines that form closed structures around an island center.

Magnetic islands are important because they can modify the transport of particles and energy across the plasma.

In tokamaks, magnetic islands are often associated with resonant magnetohydrodynamic modes and can become an important limitation on plasma confinement.

14. Stability and the Tokamak

Tokamaks must carefully control the plasma current, pressure profile, and magnetic-field configuration in order to maintain stable equilibrium.

The safety factor introduced in the previous chapter,

q(r) = rBφ / RBθ

is particularly important because the magnetic-field-line winding is related to the appearance of resonant instabilities.

The radial profile of q can therefore influence the stability of the plasma.

Tokamak stability is also strongly affected by plasma beta, current density, pressure gradients, and magnetic shear.

15. Stability and the Stellarator

Stellarators approach plasma stability differently because their magnetic geometry is produced primarily by external three-dimensional coils rather than by a large plasma current.

This reduces the dependence on current-driven confinement and eliminates the need for a large transformer-driven plasma current.

However, three-dimensional magnetic geometry introduces its own challenges. The magnetic field must be carefully optimized to maintain good confinement, reduce particle losses, and control undesirable magnetic structures.

Modern stellarators therefore rely heavily on sophisticated magnetic optimization and numerical calculations.

16. Ideal and Resistive Instabilities

Plasma instabilities can also be classified according to the physical processes required for their development.

Ideal MHD Instabilities

Ideal magnetohydrodynamic instabilities can develop under the assumption that the plasma has negligible electrical resistivity.

Examples include kink and ballooning modes.

These instabilities are primarily controlled by the balance between plasma pressure, current, and magnetic geometry.

Resistive Instabilities

Resistive instabilities require finite resistivity or other non-ideal effects.

Tearing modes are a major example.

Resistivity allows magnetic field lines to reconnect, changing the magnetic topology and potentially producing magnetic islands.

17. The Role of Magnetic Shear

Magnetic shear describes how the pitch of magnetic field lines changes from one magnetic surface to another.

A plasma with strong magnetic shear can respond differently to perturbations than one with weak magnetic shear.

Magnetic shear therefore plays an important role in determining whether certain instabilities can grow.

In tokamaks, the shear is closely related to the radial variation of the safety factor q.

Magnetic shear → variation of field-line winding → stability properties

18. Stability Is a Global Problem

One of the most important ideas in plasma physics is that stability cannot always be understood by considering a single particle.

Single-particle motion describes gyromotion and guiding-center drifts. However, plasma stability is a collective phenomenon involving enormous numbers of interacting charged particles.

Single-particle physics → Collective plasma behavior → MHD stability

This represents a transition from microscopic plasma physics to macroscopic plasma dynamics.

The behavior of the entire plasma can therefore be qualitatively different from the motion of any individual particle.

19. Why Stability Matters for Fusion

A fusion plasma must maintain high temperature and density for a sufficiently long time.

If an instability grows rapidly, the plasma can lose its desired magnetic configuration and transport energy toward the plasma edge.

In severe cases, the plasma can undergo a rapid loss of confinement.

This is why plasma stability is one of the central problems in magnetic fusion research.

The goal is not simply to create a strong magnetic field, but to construct a magnetic configuration in which a high-pressure plasma can remain both confined and stable.

20. Key Equations

The most important relations introduced in this chapter are:

J × B = ∇p
p = nekTe + nikTi
pB = B2 / 2μ0
β = 2μ0p / B2
δ(t) ∝ eγt
q(r) = rBφ / RBθ

21. Key Takeaways

  • Plasma equilibrium requires a balance between plasma pressure and electromagnetic forces.
  • A simplified MHD equilibrium condition is J × B = ∇p.
  • Magnetic pressure is given by B² / 2μ0.
  • Plasma beta measures the ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic pressure.
  • High beta is attractive for fusion because it allows greater plasma pressure for a given magnetic field.
  • However, increasing plasma pressure can also increase susceptibility to pressure-driven instabilities.
  • A plasma equilibrium can be stable, marginally stable, or unstable.
  • Kink, sausage, ballooning, and tearing modes are important examples of plasma instabilities.
  • Tearing modes can produce magnetic islands through magnetic reconnection.
  • Tokamak stability is strongly connected to plasma current, magnetic geometry, pressure gradients, and the safety-factor profile.
  • Stellarators reduce dependence on plasma current but require carefully optimized three-dimensional magnetic fields.
  • Plasma stability is a collective phenomenon and cannot be understood solely from single-particle motion.
