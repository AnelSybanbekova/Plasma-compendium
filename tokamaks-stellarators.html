Magnetic Confinement: Tokamaks and Stellarators

Introduction

One of the central challenges of fusion research is the confinement of an extremely hot plasma for a sufficiently long time. At fusion temperatures, matter cannot be placed in direct contact with ordinary material walls. Instead, magnetic fields are used to control the motion of charged particles and keep the plasma away from the surrounding structure.

The previous chapters introduced the motion of individual charged particles in magnetic fields and the guiding-center drifts that appear when magnetic fields are curved or non-uniform.

These effects become particularly important in toroidal magnetic confinement. In devices such as tokamaks and stellarators, carefully designed magnetic fields force charged particles to follow complex trajectories around a toroidal plasma.

Tokamaks and stellarators therefore represent two different solutions to the same fundamental problem:

How can a hot plasma be confined inside a closed magnetic system?

1. Why Magnetic Confinement?

Charged particles experience the Lorentz force

F = q(E + v × B)

and therefore gyrate around magnetic field lines. The characteristic radius of this motion is the Larmor radius

rL = mv⊥ / |q|B

When the Larmor radius is much smaller than the dimensions of the plasma, particles are strongly magnetized and tend to remain close to magnetic field lines.

rL ≪ L

This suggests a basic strategy for plasma confinement: construct magnetic field lines that remain inside a closed region so that charged particles can circulate without immediately reaching a material surface.

The simplest idea would be to use a long cylindrical plasma surrounded by magnetic coils. However, particles moving parallel to the magnetic field would eventually escape through the ends of the cylinder.

A natural solution is therefore to bend the cylinder into a closed ring, producing a torus.

2. Toroidal Magnetic Confinement

A torus has two important directions.

  • Toroidal direction: the long direction around the main axis of the torus.
  • Poloidal direction: the short direction around the cross-section of the plasma.

A magnetic field pointing mainly around the long direction of the torus is called the toroidal magnetic field.

At first sight, a purely toroidal magnetic field appears to solve the end-loss problem because the field lines form closed loops.

However, toroidal geometry introduces an important complication.

The magnetic field is not equally strong everywhere. It is stronger on the inner side of the torus and weaker on the outer side. Approximately,

Bφ ∝ 1 / R

where R is the distance from the major axis of the torus.

This magnetic-field gradient, together with the curvature of the magnetic field lines, produces the gradient-B and curvature drifts discussed in the previous chapter.

Electrons and ions drift in opposite directions because these magnetic drift velocities depend on the sign of the particle charge.

As a result, a simple toroidal magnetic field cannot provide sufficient confinement by itself.

3. The Need for Twisted Magnetic Field Lines

The solution is to make magnetic field lines follow a helical path rather than a purely toroidal one.

Instead of remaining permanently on the inner or outer side of the torus, a particle following a helical field line moves around both the toroidal and poloidal directions.

This allows the particle to sample different regions of the magnetic field and helps compensate for systematic drift effects.

The total magnetic field can therefore be understood as a combination of toroidal and poloidal components:

B = Bφ + Bθ

where

  • Bφ is the toroidal magnetic field,
  • Bθ is the poloidal magnetic field.

Tokamaks and stellarators both create twisted magnetic field lines, but they generate this magnetic geometry in fundamentally different ways.

4. The Tokamak

The tokamak is one of the most extensively studied magnetic confinement configurations for fusion plasmas.

Its magnetic geometry is approximately axisymmetric, meaning that the system looks essentially the same when rotated around its central axis.

A tokamak produces its helical magnetic field by combining two main magnetic components.

4.1 Toroidal Magnetic Field

The toroidal magnetic field is generated by external magnetic coils arranged around the torus.

These coils create a strong field in the toroidal direction:

Bφ

This field is typically the dominant magnetic-field component in a tokamak.

4.2 Poloidal Magnetic Field

The second component is the poloidal magnetic field.

In a tokamak, an important part of the poloidal magnetic field is generated by an electric current flowing through the plasma itself.

Ip = plasma current

This current produces a magnetic field encircling the plasma cross-section:

Ip → Bθ

The combination of the strong toroidal field and the weaker poloidal field produces helical magnetic field lines.

Bφ + Bθ → helical magnetic field

5. Magnetic Surfaces

The magnetic field lines in an ideal tokamak are organized into nested toroidal surfaces called magnetic flux surfaces.

A field line winds around the torus while remaining approximately on one of these surfaces.

Because charged particles tend to follow magnetic field lines, magnetic surfaces provide the basic structure of plasma confinement.

The innermost magnetic surface surrounds the magnetic axis, while the outer surfaces extend toward the plasma edge.

Ideally, particles and thermal energy remain confined to these magnetic surfaces for a sufficiently long time.

6. The Safety Factor

An important quantity used to describe magnetic-field-line geometry in a tokamak is the safety factor, usually denoted by q.

In a simplified circular tokamak, it can be written approximately as

q(r) = rBφ / RBθ

where

  • r is the minor radial coordinate,
  • R is the major radius of the torus,
  • Bφ is the toroidal magnetic field,
  • Bθ is the poloidal magnetic field.

Physically, the safety factor describes how magnetic field lines wind around the torus.

It is closely related to the number of toroidal turns made by a field line for each poloidal turn.

The radial profile q(r) plays an important role in plasma equilibrium and stability. Certain values of q can be associated with resonant magnetic surfaces and magnetohydrodynamic instabilities.

A detailed discussion of these instabilities belongs to plasma equilibrium and stability, which naturally follows the present chapter.

7. Plasma Current in a Tokamak

The plasma current is one of the defining features of a tokamak.

Traditionally, it is induced using transformer action. The plasma behaves approximately like the secondary winding of a transformer, while a central solenoid changes the magnetic flux through the torus.

According to Faraday's law,

∮ E · dl = − dΦB / dt

the changing magnetic flux produces an electric field around the torus, driving a toroidal plasma current.

This current has two important consequences:

  • it produces part of the poloidal magnetic field,
  • it also contributes to ohmic heating of the plasma.

However, transformer-driven current cannot naturally remain constant forever. This makes continuous steady-state operation more difficult for tokamaks and motivates the development of non-inductive current-drive techniques.

8. Advantages and Challenges of Tokamaks

Tokamaks have several important advantages.

  • Their axisymmetric geometry is relatively simple compared with fully three-dimensional magnetic configurations.
  • They have demonstrated excellent plasma confinement.
  • A very large experimental and theoretical knowledge base has been developed around tokamak physics.

However, tokamaks also face significant challenges.

  • A large plasma current must be generated and controlled.
  • The plasma current can drive magnetohydrodynamic instabilities.
  • Sudden losses of plasma confinement, known as disruptions, can occur.
  • Maintaining continuous steady-state operation is difficult.
  • Particles and energy can still escape through turbulent and collisional transport.

9. The Stellarator

The stellarator provides a different approach to toroidal magnetic confinement.

Like a tokamak, a stellarator confines plasma using twisted magnetic field lines organized around a toroidal geometry.

The crucial difference is how this twist is produced.

Instead of relying primarily on a large toroidal plasma current to generate the poloidal magnetic field, a stellarator creates most of its required magnetic geometry using external three-dimensional coils.

The coils themselves are carefully shaped so that the magnetic field lines wind helically around the torus.

10. Three-Dimensional Magnetic Geometry

Unlike the approximately axisymmetric tokamak, a stellarator generally has a fully three-dimensional magnetic configuration.

The magnetic coils may have complex non-planar shapes. Their geometry is designed so that the resulting field forms nested magnetic surfaces capable of confining the plasma.

This makes stellarators more complicated to design and manufacture. However, it also provides considerable freedom to optimize the magnetic configuration.

Modern stellarator design relies heavily on numerical optimization to reduce particle losses, improve magnetic surfaces, and control plasma transport.

11. Rotational Transform

The twisting of magnetic field lines in a stellarator is often described using the rotational transform, denoted by ι.

Conceptually, the rotational transform describes how much a magnetic field line advances in the poloidal direction as it travels around the torus.

The rotational transform is closely related to the tokamak safety factor. Using a common convention,

ι = 1 / q

although exact definitions and normalization conventions can vary.

In a tokamak, much of this rotational transform is associated with the plasma current. In a stellarator, it can be produced largely by the external magnetic coils.

12. Why Stellarators Are Interesting

One of the major advantages of the stellarator concept is that it does not require a large net toroidal plasma current for its basic magnetic configuration.

This gives stellarators several attractive properties.

  • They are naturally suited to steady-state operation.
  • They are less dependent on transformer-driven plasma current.
  • The risk of large current-driven disruptions can be reduced.
  • Their magnetic geometry can be optimized using external coils.

However, stellarators also present major challenges.

  • Their magnetic geometry is fully three-dimensional.
  • The coils can be extremely complex to manufacture and assemble.
  • Small construction errors can affect the magnetic surfaces.
  • Particle transport in three-dimensional fields requires careful optimization.

13. Tokamak vs Stellarator

Tokamaks and stellarators use the same basic physical principle: charged particles are confined by strong, twisted magnetic fields.

Their main difference lies in the origin of the magnetic-field twist.

Property Tokamak Stellarator
Geometry Approximately axisymmetric Fully three-dimensional
Toroidal magnetic field External coils External coils
Magnetic-field twist Strongly associated with plasma current Primarily produced by shaped external coils
Large plasma current Required Not required for the basic configuration
Steady-state operation More difficult Natural advantage
Disruptions Important concern Generally less severe concern
Coil design Relatively simpler Much more geometrically complex

14. Magnetic Islands

In an ideal confinement system, magnetic surfaces are smooth and nested. Real magnetic fields, however, can contain perturbations.

Near certain resonant surfaces, these perturbations can modify the magnetic topology and create structures known as magnetic islands.

Inside a magnetic island, field lines form a different pattern from the surrounding nested flux surfaces.

Large magnetic islands can increase radial transport and degrade plasma confinement.

If several magnetic islands overlap strongly, the magnetic field can become partially stochastic, allowing particles and energy to move more easily across the plasma.

Magnetic islands are important in both tokamak and stellarator physics and provide another connection between magnetic geometry, plasma transport, and plasma stability.

15. From Single-Particle Motion to Fusion Devices

Tokamaks and stellarators demonstrate how the microscopic physics discussed in the previous chapters becomes part of a macroscopic confinement system.

A charged particle first undergoes gyromotion around a magnetic field line. Its guiding center then experiences gradient-B, curvature, and electric-field drifts.

In a toroidal device, the magnetic configuration must be designed so that these motions do not lead to rapid particle loss.

Gyromotion → Drifts → Magnetic Surfaces → Plasma Confinement

The geometry of the magnetic field therefore determines how well individual particle trajectories combine to produce confinement of the plasma as a whole.

16. Key Equations

The most important relations introduced in this chapter are summarized below.

F = q(E + v × B)
rL = mv⊥ / |q|B
Bφ ∝ 1 / R
B = Bφ + Bθ
q(r) = rBφ / RBθ
ι = 1 / q
∮ E · dl = − dΦB / dt

17. Summary

  • Magnetic confinement uses the gyromotion of charged particles to restrict their motion across magnetic field lines.
  • Closing a magnetic system into a torus removes the simple end losses of a linear magnetic device.
  • A purely toroidal magnetic field is insufficient because field curvature and magnetic-field gradients generate particle drifts.
  • Successful toroidal confinement therefore requires twisted magnetic field lines.
  • A tokamak combines an externally generated toroidal magnetic field with a poloidal field strongly associated with the plasma current.
  • The combination of toroidal and poloidal magnetic fields creates helical field lines and nested magnetic surfaces.
  • The tokamak safety factor q describes the winding of magnetic field lines.
  • A stellarator creates the required three-dimensional magnetic geometry primarily using external coils.
  • Stellarators do not require a large net plasma current for their fundamental confinement configuration and are therefore naturally attractive for steady-state operation.
  • Tokamaks have simpler geometry but must control plasma current and current-driven instabilities.
  • Stellarators avoid some of these problems at the cost of much more complex magnetic geometry and coil design.

Connection to the Next Topic

Constructing closed magnetic surfaces is only one part of the confinement problem.

A magnetically confined plasma contains pressure gradients, electric currents, magnetic-field curvature, and enormous amounts of thermal energy. These effects can disturb the equilibrium and generate instabilities.

The next step is therefore to understand how a plasma reaches force balance and under what conditions that equilibrium remains stable.
